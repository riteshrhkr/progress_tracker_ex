This document is about *delivery*, not knowledge. It assumes you already study the topics (OOP, collections, Spring, Kafka). The gap it closes is this: you know the answer, then freeze, ramble, or talk around the point.

Index of related study notes lives elsewhere (`java/oops`, `java/jmm`, `spring/`). This file is the how-to for the room.

toc::[]

---

== The gap

Knowing a topic and explaining it under pressure are different skills.

A weak delivery sounds like: "So HashMap is like... it stores data... the hash function..."

A strong delivery sounds like: "HashMap is a key-value store. It uses hashing so average lookup is O(1). Use it for a cache keyed by user id. It is not thread-safe — for shared maps use ConcurrentHashMap."

The rest of this guide is how to get from the first to the second.

=== Key takeaways

* Classify the question before you speak.
* Lead with the answer, then explain, then give one example.
* Silence is better than filler.

---

== Classify the question first

If you misread the type, you answer the wrong thing confidently. That is worse than not knowing.

=== Technical

[cols="1,2,2"]
|===
|Type |What they want |Signal words

|Definition
|What it is, in one breath
|"What is...", "Define...", "Explain..."

|Comparison
|When to pick which, not a list of facts
|"Difference between...", "vs", "When would you use..."

|Internal working
|A flow from start to finish
|"How does... work?", "What happens when...", "Walk me through..."

|Scenario / design
|Your reasoning, then a recommendation
|"How would you design...", "If you had to...", "Suppose..."

|Debugging
|How you find the bottleneck, not a guess
|"How would you debug...", "What could cause...", "Why is this failing?"

|Code
|What the method does, then the walkthrough
|"Write...", "Implement...", "Show me..."

|Opinion / trade-off
|A position, reasons, and what you give up
|"What do you prefer?", "What are the trade-offs?"
|===

=== Non-technical / HR

[cols="1,2,2"]
|===
|Type |What they want |Signal words

|Behavioral
|A real past story, with *your* actions
|"Tell me about a time...", "Give an example..."

|Situational
|What you would do next time
|"What would you do if...", "How would you handle..."

|Self-assessment
|Honest strengths and one real weakness plus a fix
|"Tell me about yourself", "What is your weakness?"

|Motivational
|Why this role and this company, specific
|"Why us?", "Why this role?"

|Conflict / collaboration
|Maturity, not blame
|"How do you handle conflict?", "Describe a difficult colleague..."

|Failure / learning
|What changed after the mistake
|"Tell me about a failure", "What would you do differently?"
|===

=== Key takeaways

* Name the type in your head in two seconds.
* Comparison is "when to use which". Internal working is "first, then, finally".
* Behavioral is STAR. Most of the time goes to what *you* did.

== Think before you speak

The job of your brain in those 3–5 seconds is not to recite. It is to pick a structure.

=== Definition

Pause two seconds. Can you say it in one sentence? Then: what it is → why it exists → one example.

Mental cue: "What is it? What does it do? Where is it used?"

=== Comparison

Name both things. Ask: what problem does each solve? Speak: main difference → when to pick A → when to pick B.

Never only list differences. Always say when each is the right choice.

Mental cue: "Same family, different job."

=== Internal working

Picture a timeline. Input → steps → output → one gotcha (thread safety, exceptions, a version change).

Speak in order. Do not jump around.

Mental cue: "Trace it from start to end."

=== Scenario / design

Restate the problem in your own words. Name constraints (scale, consistency, latency). List options, then pick one and say what you give up.

Do not jump to a solution. Show the thinking.

Mental cue: "Clarify → options → trade-off → pick."

=== Debugging

Reproduce, isolate, fix, then prevent. Do not tune the JVM until you know *where* time is spent.

Mental cue: "Where is the bottleneck, then why."

=== Behavioral

STAR: Situation → Task → Action → Result.

Pick the first relevant story. Spend about 70% of the answer on Action. Close with a result or a learning, even if the outcome was messy.

Mental cue: "STAR, and Action is the long part."

=== Opinion

State your view in sentence one. Give two reasons. Name the trade-off. Do not say "it depends" unless you say *what* it depends on.

Mental cue: "Take a side, justify it, admit the cost."

=== Self-assessment

Strength: one that matches the role, plus proof.

Weakness: a real one, then what you are doing about it. Never "I work too hard."

=== Key takeaways

* Three to five seconds of silence is planning, not failure.
* Each type has a tiny script. Use it.
* "It depends" without the condition is an empty answer.

== Answer shapes

These are mental templates, not scripts to memorize.

[cols="1,3"]
|===
|Type |Flow

|Definition
|What it is → key property → short example

|Comparison
|Both in one line → main difference → when to use which

|Internal working
|One-line overview → step by step → result → one edge case

|Scenario
|Restate → options → trade-offs → recommendation

|Debugging
|Reproduce → isolate → fix → stop it happening again

|Behavioral
|Situation → Task → Action → Result (STAR)

|Self-assessment
|Honest statement → evidence → growth or mitigation

|Opinion
|My view is... → reason → reason → what you give up

|Motivation
|Short reason → why this team → what you bring
|===

=== The fallback when you are unsure of the type

. One-line answer (the core point)
. Brief why or how
. One concrete example
. One-line close (impact or recommendation)

That is also the *3-point rule*: core idea, how or why, example. Three points feel complete. Six points feel like a dump.

=== Open with the answer

Wrong: "So, uh, basically HashMap is... you know, it stores key-value pairs and..."

Right: "HashMap is a key-value store. It finds the bucket with a hash. For a user cache, get-by-id is O(1) on average."

=== Key takeaways

* Start with the answer, not the story of how you thought of it.
* Three points, then stop. Offer to go deeper.
* One example per point.

== How to recall when you blank

Blanking is normal. The fix is not more memorizing. It is better triggers.

=== Keyword anchors

Before interviews, shrink each concept to a 3–5 word phrase.

* HashMap → "key-value, hash-bucket, O(1) average"
* Spring IoC → "container manages beans, DI"
* STAR → "situation, task, action, result"

When you blank, say the anchor in your head. The rest usually follows.

=== Chunking

Group related ideas in threes. Recall the group name, then the three items. Do not try to recall a long list.

----
Spring Core        = IoC + DI + bean lifecycle
Java Collections   = List, Set, Map (then the main implementations)
Microservices win  = scale independently, isolate failure, deploy separately
----

=== Story titles, not memorized speeches

For behavioral questions, remember titles, then apply STAR:

----
- "The deadline crunch"     (pressure)
- "The team disagreement"   (conflict)
- "The production bug"      (problem solving)
- "The failed sprint"       (failure / learning)
----

Scan titles → pick the closest → tell it with STAR.

=== Start talking from what you know

Silence grows panic. Partial answers grow recall.

----
"Let me think through this..."
"At a high level, X is about..."
"One way to approach this would be..."
----

If they allow a whiteboard, draw boxes and arrows. Spatial memory often returns what words will not.

=== Key takeaways

* Anchors beat paragraphs.
* Chunks of three beat lists of ten.
* Start speaking. Silence is the enemy.

== How to speak

[cols="1,3"]
|===
|Rule |How

|Start with the answer
|"The main difference is X" — not "both are similar in some ways..."

|One idea per sentence
|Short sentences survive nerves. Compound clauses do not.

|No filler
|Drop "basically", "like", "you know", "kind of", "sort of", "um"

|Pause instead
|One second of quiet sounds confident. "Umm so basically" does not.

|Speak at about 80% of your normal speed
|Nerves speed you up. Slow down on purpose.

|End sentences down, not up
|A falling tone is a statement. A rising tone sounds like a question.

|Signal turns
|"First...", "Second...", "To close..."
|===

=== Stop rambling

Set an exit in your head before you start. One point, one example, stop.

If you have said six sentences, close with: "That is the key idea. I can go deeper on any part."

Rambling is usually a structure problem, not a length problem.

=== When you do not know

Never go silent. Never say "I don't know" and stop.

----
"I have not used that feature directly.
 Based on how [related thing] works,
 I would expect [inference]."
----

Or:

----
"I am not certain of the exact internals.
 The idea I do know is [what you know]."
----

Reasoning from a neighbor concept is what they are scoring.

If you are guessing: "I believe it is X. I would verify before using it in production."

=== Key takeaways

* Pause beats filler.
* Slow down.
* Infer from what you know. Do not freeze.

=== Technical

[cols="1,2,2"]

|Mistake |Sounds like |Fix

|Three points, then offer more

|"It depends" with no condition
|"It depends."
|"It depends on whether you need X or Y."
|===

== Worked examples

Each example shows the type, the two-second plan, then the spoken answer. Steal the shape, not the wording. Use your own systems and stories.

=== Definition — What is Dependency Injection?

Plan: what it is → why → Spring → one example.

----
Dependency Injection means an object does not build its own helpers.
Someone else hands them in. That keeps classes from knowing how to
construct a database or a client.

In Spring, the container does that job: constructor, setter, or field.

Example: a Service does not `new` a Repository. Spring injects the Repository.
Tests can pass a fake one. That is the point — loose coupling, easier tests.
----

=== Definition — What is a Spring bean?

----
A Spring bean is an object the IoC container creates, wires, and destroys.

You mark it with `@Component`, `@Service`, `@Repository`, or a `@Bean` method.
You stop calling `new` for those objects. The container owns the lifecycle.
----

=== Definition — What is the IoC container?

----
The IoC container is Spring's core. It creates beans, injects dependencies,
and manages scope.

Inversion of Control means the framework decides when objects are born,
not your `main` method.

It reads annotations or `@Configuration`, builds the graph, then your
`@Service` appears wherever it is needed.
----

=== Comparison — `@Component` vs `@Service` vs `@Repository` vs `@Controller`

----
All four register a Spring bean. All are specializations of `@Component`.

The names tell the layer, and one of them does extra work:
  - `@Component` — generic bean, no special role
  - `@Service` — business logic
  - `@Repository` — persistence, plus exception translation to Spring's DataAccessException
  - `@Controller` / `@RestController` — web layer, request mapping

Use the specific one so the next reader (and Spring) know the layer.
----

=== Comparison — `@Component` vs `@Bean`

----
`@Component` sits on a class you own. Spring finds it by scanning.

`@Bean` sits on a method in a `@Configuration` class. You build the object
yourself. Use it when you cannot annotate the class — third-party types,
or a Jackson `ObjectMapper` with custom setup.
----

=== Comparison — ArrayList vs LinkedList

----
ArrayList is a growable array. LinkedList is a doubly linked list.

Get-by-index: ArrayList is O(1). LinkedList is O(n).
Insert in the middle: LinkedList is O(1) only if you already hold the node.
ArrayList uses contiguous memory, so it is usually faster in real reads.

Default to ArrayList. Use LinkedList only when you truly walk and splice
nodes and almost never jump by index.
----

=== Comparison — HashMap vs ConcurrentHashMap

----
HashMap is not safe for concurrent writes. Two threads can corrupt it
or, in older versions, livelock in a resize.

ConcurrentHashMap is built for concurrent reads and writes. Java 8+ uses
CAS and fine-grained locking, not a single lock on the whole map.

Single-threaded cache: HashMap is simpler and a bit faster.
Shared cache across threads: ConcurrentHashMap. Do not wrap HashMap in
`Collections.synchronizedMap` and hope — you still need to lock on
compound operations like check-then-put.
----

=== Internal working — How does HashMap work?

Plan: structure → put → get → collision → resize.

----
HashMap is an array of buckets.

put(key, value):
  1. Call hashCode() on the key
  2. Turn that hash into a bucket index
  3. Store the entry there

If two keys land in the same bucket, Java 8+ keeps a linked list, then
turns it into a tree once that bucket has enough nodes (8, and the table
is large enough).

get(key): hash → bucket → walk with equals() until the key matches.

Average is O(1). If every key collides, you degrade toward O(n).
When the map passes load factor 0.75, the table doubles and entries rehash.
----

=== Internal working — How does `@Transactional` work?

----
Spring sees `@Transactional` and wraps the bean in a proxy (JDK or CGLIB).

A call from another bean hits the proxy first: begin transaction, call
the real method, commit on success, roll back on an unchecked exception
(by default).

Gotcha: a method in the same class calling another `@Transactional`
method on `this` skips the proxy. No transaction starts.

Self-invocation is the classic production bug. Split the class or
inject self through the container if you must.
----

=== Internal working — What happens when Spring Boot starts?

----
SpringApplication.run() roughly:

  1. Build SpringApplication
  2. Create the application context
  3. Scan `@Component` and `@Bean`
  4. Create beans, inject dependencies
  5. Run `@PostConstruct`
  6. Publish ApplicationReadyEvent
  7. Start the embedded server (often Tomcat)

Auto-configuration fills defaults from what is on the classpath.
You can see the report with `--debug` or the condition evaluation report.
----

=== Scenario — Design a REST API for banking

----
I would clarify first: reporting only, or money movement?

Entities: Customer, Account, Transaction.

Endpoints:
  GET  /accounts/{id}
  POST /accounts/{id}/transfers
  GET  /transactions?accountId=

Must-haves for transfers:
  - Idempotency key on POST so a retry does not double-pay
  - A real database transaction around debit and credit
  - Auth (OAuth2 / JWT), HTTPS
  - Standard HTTP codes and an error body

Start thin. Add pagination, rate limits, and audit logs when the
product needs them — not on day one.
----

=== Debugging — The service is slow

----
1. Is it always slow, or only sometimes?
2. Metrics: CPU, memory, thread pools, GC pauses, DB time.
3. Trace one request (OpenTelemetry, Zipkin). Find the slow hop.
4. Usual causes: N+1 queries, pool exhaustion, chatty HTTP, GC thrash.
5. Fix that cause. Add an alert so it is not a surprise next time.

Do not change JVM flags until you know the hop.
----

=== Opinion — REST vs Kafka

----
I use HTTP when the caller needs an answer now: fetch a user, confirm a payment.

I use Kafka when work is async: fan-out events, high volume, producers
should not wait on every consumer.

Cost of HTTP: simpler, but services couple on availability.
Cost of Kafka: replay and scale, plus more ops (partitions, consumer groups).

Sync request-response inside the org: HTTP.
Events and workflows that can lag: Kafka.
----

=== Behavioral — Production incident

----
Situation: Peak traffic, payment service returning 500s, about 15% of
checkouts failing.

Task: I was on call. SLA was 30 minutes to restore.

Action: Dashboards showed the DB pool saturated. The last deploy added a
query without an index. I rolled that query back, added the index in a
migration, redeployed.

Result: Recovered in 18 minutes. I wrote a postmortem and added a
query-plan check in CI so the same miss would fail the build.
----

=== Behavioral — Conflict

----
Situation: A teammate and I disagreed: third-party cache library vs custom.

Task: Sprint started the next day. We needed a call.

Action: I did not escalate first. I asked we each write a one-pager of
trade-offs and walk the team through them. His option was more mature.
Mine fit our API better. We used the library and wrapped it.

Result: We shipped. The team still uses "write it down, compare, decide."
I learned most fights are missing information, not ego.
----

Keep Situation short. Name *your* steps. End with outcome plus a habit that lasted.

=== Self-assessment — Tell me about yourself

Not a resume. About 60–90 seconds.

----
Current role → one strength with a number or a shipped thing →
why this team, named specifically.
----

Example shape:

----
I am a Java backend engineer. At [Company] I build Spring Boot services
on Kafka.

I care about making hot paths honest — last year we cut order latency
about 40% by fixing the async pipeline, not by adding machines.

I want this role because you run this at a scale I have not owned yet,
especially the consumer-group and partition work. That is the direction
I am aiming at.
----

Fill with your facts. Do not copy the numbers.

=== Self-assessment — Weakness

Real weakness → how it hurt → what you changed.

----
I used to polish a design too long before showing anyone.

That delayed feedback.

I now time-box: two hours, then a draft, even if it is ugly. Early
review has been better every time. The habit is still work. I am
getting faster at sharing early.
----

Another honest shape: over-building flexibility. Mitigation: YAGNI, and
a five-minute check with the person who asked for the feature before you
dig.

=== Key takeaways

* Plan in two seconds, then speak the template.
* Technical: definition, difference, or flow — pick one.
* Stories: I did X, result was Y.

== Practice

Knowledge without speaking is not interview-ready. Practice out loud.

=== Daily 30 minutes

[cols="1,2,2"]
|===
|Time |Do this |Why

|5 min
|One concept → write a 3–5 word anchor
|Recall trigger

|10 min
|One technical answer out loud, 90 second timer
|Structure and length

|10 min
|One STAR story, 2 minute timer
|Fluency

|5 min
|Did I start with the answer? Example? Close?
|Self-coach
|===

If you only have 15 minutes: 10 technical + 5 behavioral.

=== Drill 1 — 30-second definition

Timer on. Five concepts a day:

----
"[Concept] is [one sentence].
 It works by [short mechanism].
 For example, [one example]."
----

If you cannot finish, you do not know it yet. Study, then retry.

=== Drill 2 — Comparison flip

Two related things. One line each, under ten words, for *when to use*.

----
HashMap  → unordered, need average O(1)
TreeMap  → you need sorted keys
----

=== Drill 3 — One concept, three audiences

Same topic, three tellings: junior (analogy), peer (precise), manager (what breaks in prod if we get it wrong). If the three collapse into one speech, you are still reciting.

=== Drill 4 — Silent start

Question. Three seconds of nothing. Classify. Then open with the core point. This kills panic-ramble.

=== Drill 5 — "So what?"

After a practice answer: why does anyone care? If you cannot say it, add one sentence of impact.

=== Drill 6 — Record a mock

Once a week, 45 minutes. Mix technical and behavioral. Record on your phone. Watch for fillers, length, and whether you led with the answer. Watching yourself once beats a lot of silent reading.

Short mock if time is tight:

[cols="1,2"]
|===
|Block |Content

|10 min
|Three technical: definition, comparison, internals

|5 min
|One scenario + one trade-off

|5 min
|Intro + one STAR + one weakness

|After
|What was fuzzy. Fix those five things next week.
|===

=== Weekly rhythm

[cols="1,3"]
|===
|Week |Focus

|1
|Question types + answer shapes until they are automatic

|2
|Five STAR stories written and spoken

|3
|Definitions and comparison drills every day

|4
|Full mocks and recordings

|After
|One technical deep dive a week. Refresh stories monthly.
|===

Sunday review: five concepts you fumbled, restudy, 30-second rule, add one story if you lived one.

=== Key takeaways

* Out loud, with a timer.
* Record once a week.
* Consistency beats a weekend cram.

* Answer first
* One example, specific
* Close with impact or a recommendation
* Pause instead of filler
* Ask one clarifying question when the scenario is vague

== Final takeaways

. Interviews score *how you think out loud*, not how many pages you read.
. Classify → one-line answer → two or three supports → example → stop.
. Technical: happy path, then the gotcha. Comparison: when to choose which.
. Behavioral: one STAR story, mostly Action, always a Result.
. Blanking: start from the keyword you do know.
. A clear 70% answer beats a rambling 100% answer.
. Practice out loud. Daily. That is the actual shortcut.

Quick self-check before a loop:

* Can I define HashMap, DI, and `@Transactional` in 30 seconds each?
* Can I compare HashMap vs ConcurrentHashMap and REST vs Kafka with "when"?
* Do I have five titled STAR stories I can tell in under two minutes?
* Did I record myself this week?
